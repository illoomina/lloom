The transition from sail to steam in commercial shipping is often told as a single displacement, with steam arriving and sail retreating, but the record of tonnage registered between 1850 and 1900 does not support that shape. Sailing tonnage continued to grow in absolute terms for three decades after the first regular steam services, and on several long-haul routes it grew faster than steam did. The reason is that the two technologies were not competing for the same cargoes. Steam was expensive per ton-mile because it carried its own fuel, and on a voyage from Liverpool to Melbourne a steamer of the 1860s would surrender a large fraction of its hold to coal, refilling at bunkering stations whose owners understood their position perfectly well. Sail carried nothing but cargo. For dense, non-perishable, low-value freight where three additional weeks at sea cost the shipper little, the economics favoured sail decisively, and they continued to favour it until the compound engine and later the triple-expansion engine cut coal consumption enough to change the arithmetic.

What altered the balance was therefore not the invention of the steamship but a sequence of incremental improvements in thermal efficiency, each of which moved the break-even distance further out. The opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 mattered for the same reason rather than as an independent cause: it removed a long passage on which sail was strong and replaced it with a short one on which a steamer could reach a bunkering station without giving up its hold. Historians who treat the canal as the decisive event tend to be reasoning from route maps rather than from fuel curves. The nitrate and grain trades, which had no comparable shortcut, kept large sailing vessels profitable into the 1920s, and the last commercial sailing cargoes were carried not by nostalgic owners but by operators who had done the arithmetic and found it still worked.
